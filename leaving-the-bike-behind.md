# Leaving the Bike Behind

## A country-by-country report on parking an Australian-registered motorcycle between Sydney and London, flying out, and coming back

**Prepared 26 July 2026**
**Assumed trip window: departure 2026–27, arrival in Europe 2027–28**
**Vehicle: motorcycle registered in New South Wales (NSW), Australia, owner-ridden, Carnet de Passages en Douane (CPD) carried**

---

## How to read this report

Your central question was: *can I leave my bike in a country, fly home to Australia or go somewhere else, and come back to it later — for how long, how, and how easily?*

The research produced an answer that is more useful than a simple yes or no, because the question turns out to contain **three separate questions** that almost every forum thread and government page conflates. Getting them apart is the single most valuable thing in this document, so it comes first, before the tables.

Everything is tagged with a confidence level. I have used the same four labels throughout, and they mean specific things:

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **HIGH** | Two or more independent sources agree, including either an official/government source or several recent (2023+) firsthand rider accounts. |
| **MEDIUM** | Recent firsthand accounts agree but no official confirmation, **or** a clear official rule with no recent evidence of how it is actually enforced. |
| **LOW** | A single source, or sources five or more years old, or sources that actively conflict. |
| **UNKNOWN** | Nothing found. Where I say UNKNOWN I mean the research genuinely came up empty, not that I ran out of interest. |

A word on the evidence base, because you should know its shape. Ten research teams worked this, leaning on Horizons Unlimited (the HUBB), ADVrider, Caravanistan, GT-Rider, Ride Asia, iOverlander, Reddit, Mad or Nomad, Team-BHP, blogs and ride reports, cross-checked against customs authorities, transport ministries, EUR-Lex, gov.uk, Turkish and Greek tax authorities, Australia's Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade (DFAT) and equivalent advisories. Three structural problems recurred and you should factor them in:

**Caravanistan blocks automated reading.** It is the single best source on Iran, Pakistan and Central Asia and it has threads with titles like *"Long-term vehicle storage in Eurasian Customs Union Q&A"* and *"Parking a vehicle in Iran on a carnet de passage"* that go directly to your question. Nobody could open them. **Read those yourself. They are the highest-value unread material in existence for this trip.** ADVrider similarly returns a paywall to automated access.

**The good news about your question is mostly old.** Almost every favourable firsthand account of parking a bike and flying home is from 2012–2019. The Kyrgyz accounts predate that country's 2015 accession to the Eurasian Economic Union. The European "nobody ever noticed" accounts are 2012–2016. Since 2020 Greece has digitised and started immobilising foreign-plated vehicles in operations netting hundreds of cars, Kosovo has begun recording vehicle entries, Montenegro is building a Foreign Vehicle Register, Kyrgyzstan imposed a new six-month cap in May 2025 and temporary state registration in October 2025, and India's equivalent of Pakistan's Federal Board of Revenue (FBR) tightened monitoring. **Do not forecast 2027 enforcement from 2015 non-enforcement.**

**Your specific use case is close to unprecedented on the current route.** Because Myanmar and the Wagah border are shut, essentially nobody is riding a foreign bike overland into India any more, and the freight-in cohort is tiny. In several countries you will be doing something for which no documented precedent could be found. That is not a reason not to do it. It is a reason to get things in writing.

## Part 1 — The three questions

### Question 1: Can I physically and legally leave the country without my bike?

**Almost everywhere, yes.** This is the question people ask, and it is the least interesting of the three. In the overwhelming majority of countries on your route there is no immigration check that links a departing passenger to an open vehicle-import record. The vehicle sits in a customs system; you walk through an immigration system; the two do not talk. Riders have flown out of Ulaanbaatar, Almaty, Bangkok, Vientiane, Athens and Antalya leaving bikes behind, and nobody stopped them at the gate.

There are exactly **four significant exceptions** on the whole route:

- **Turkey**, where the vehicle is written into your passport record and the customs system, and leaving without formally notifying customs is a defined offence with a defined fine. *(HIGH)*
- **India**, where at least one specialist source states the re-export date is endorsed in the passport at clearance and a tourist may not leave the vehicle "unattended or with a friend or relative" — it must go to a bonded warehouse. *(MEDIUM, and contested — see the India section)*
- **China and Turkmenistan**, where you never had the option, because the bike only entered on a fixed-date escorted transit permit and there is no mechanism for it to remain after the permit party leaves. *(HIGH)*
- **Pakistan**, where the one source that addresses it directly says leaving without your vehicle is "not usually permitted" and requires notifying the Board of Revenue. *(MEDIUM, no firsthand accounts found at all)*

### Question 2: Does the clock stop while I'm gone?

**Almost nowhere.** This is the question that actually governs your trip, and it is the one nobody asks.

Every country grants your bike a period of temporary admission — 30 days, 90 days, six months, a year. That period is **calendar time from the moment of import**. It does not care whether the bike is being ridden across a desert or sitting under a dust sheet in a Chiang Mai lockup while you are at a wedding back in Australia. The European Union's rule is the clearest statement of the general principle: Delegated Regulation 2015/2446 Article 217 measures the period "from when goods are placed under the procedure," and the UK's implementing instrument says "beginning with the date the goods are declared." The widespread forum belief that the clock counts "time on the road, not storage" is folklore with no legal support anywhere. *(HIGH)*

So the real constraint on how long you can leave the bike is not the garage, the security, or the insurance. **It is the remaining balance on a permit that is quietly draining the entire time you are away.** A two-month trip home eats two months of a 90-day Turkish allowance. A six-month Australian working stint exhausts almost any permit on the route.

There are only **five mechanisms anywhere on the route that genuinely stop or reset the clock**, and knowing them is the practical core of this report:

1. **Ride the bike out of the country (or out of the customs territory) before you fly.** Crude, cheap, completely reliable, and requires you to be physically present. This is the workhorse.
2. **A customs bonded warehouse.** Legally bulletproof, available in principle in most jurisdictions, and priced for freight rather than for travellers — port bonded storage runs around USD 28/day at Laem Chabang and was reported at ~AUD 200/day at an Australian port. Rational for weeks, absurd for months. *(MEDIUM)*
3. **Turkey's surrender-to-customs route** (*yediemin otoparkı* / customs custody), which Turkish sources say holds the vehicle for three months extendable to six, with the clock suspended and the passport annotation closed. Cost unknown. *(MEDIUM — and see the caveat in the Turkey section, because this is contested)*
4. **Greece's immobilisation procedure.** The one genuine surprise of this research. Greece has a digital, officially-sanctioned, no-customs-officer-required process via myAADE (the online portal of AADE, Greece's Independent Authority for Public Revenue) to immobilise a non-EU vehicle in private parking for **a minimum of six and a maximum of twenty-four months**. It is the only mechanism in the EU designed for exactly your problem. *(HIGH)*
5. **India's "double lock" garaging** — premises approved by the Commissioner of Customs with Customs holding one of two keys. Real, in the actual rule text, but written for repairs and hospitalisation, and there is no account of anyone using it to go on holiday. *(HIGH that the provision exists; MEDIUM at best that it is available to you)*

### Question 3: Can the paperwork be renewed while I'm away?

**Usually not, and this is where trips die.** Extension of a temporary import almost universally requires the holder to present themselves, the vehicle, or both, at a customs office. Tajikistan's 15-day permit is extendable in Dushanbe or Khorog — which is useless if you are in Sydney. The Eurasian Economic Union (EAEU) rule requires temporarily imported goods to remain "in actual possession and use of the customs declarant," which cuts against proxy renewal. Whether an agent or a notarised power of attorney works is one of the largest unresolved questions in this whole report and no source answered it. *(UNKNOWN, and it matters enormously)*

The carnet itself is the exception, and it is good news: a carnet **can** be renewed while you and the bike are abroad, through the club appointed by the AIT/FIA (the Alliance Internationale de Tourisme and the Fédération Internationale de l'Automobile, the two international motoring bodies that administer the carnet system) in whichever country the bike is currently sitting, which liaises with local customs on your behalf. *(HIGH)*

### The synthesis, in one paragraph

Your trip is not constrained by where you can store a bike — storage is cheap, plentiful and, in Chiang Mai, better organised than in most of Australia. It is constrained by a rule you cannot see and nobody will remind you of: **the permit keeps burning.** Design the trip so that every flight home happens either (a) well inside a long permit with margin to spare, (b) immediately after riding the bike across a border into a fresh jurisdiction, or (c) in one of the four or five places on earth that have built a formal door for this — Greece above all, Turkey with caveats, a bonded warehouse if you are desperate. Everything else in this report is detail.

### The tier list that falls out of the table

**Tier 1 — build the trip around these.**
**Georgia** is the best place on the entire route to leave a motorcycle, and it wins on an unusual mechanism: the overstay penalty is 50 GEL per day but **capped at 1,000 GEL in total**. Your maximum exposure for leaving the bike indefinitely is about AUD 550, with no escalating cliff, no confiscation trigger, and no procedure to file. Add a year of visa-free stay for Australians, Tbilisi air freight, and Poti and Batumi a few hours away, and it is the answer. *(HIGH)*

**Greece** is second and is the right answer for a genuinely long stop, because it is the only jurisdiction on the route with a purpose-built legal door: immobilise the bike through myAADE in private parking, no customs officer attends, and the clock stops for six to twenty-four months. The price is that immobilisation locks the bike for a **minimum** of six months, so it is a "sit out a year" tool, not a "back in three weeks" tool. *(HIGH)*

**Thailand** is third and is the best short-to-medium answer in Asia. Chiang Mai has at least four named commercial storage businesses charging AUD 40–70 a month, one of them seven kilometres from the airport, several run by overlanders who advertise exactly this service. The legal position is worse than the infrastructure — 60 permitted days per calendar year on the DLT permit — but the documented downside is a fine capped at 10,000 THB, and one rider left a bike in Pran Buri for **two years** and paid 1,000 THB. *(HIGH)*

**Tier 2 — works, with a specific catch.**
**Malaysia** (evidenced six-month storage, real storage-plus-freight-plus-brokerage under one roof in Kuala Lumpur, but a 180-day-per-year ICP ceiling whose interaction with a parked bike is unresolved). **Albania** (12 months, cheap online insurance, outside the EU customs territory so it stops the EU clock — but the 12-month figure comes from the general temporary-admission article, not a vehicle-specific rule). **Mongolia** (genuinely easy, and completely undermined by the cost of getting a bike there). **Nepal** (eight months observed, but the brand-new TIV system caps non-carnet stays at 30 days per fiscal year and nobody has tested a foreign carnet under it).

**Tier 3 — only with paperwork you have arranged in advance.**
**Turkey** (legal, documented, but the free option doesn't stop the clock and the clock-stopping option means surrendering the bike to an impound for a minimum you can't control). **India** (the longest permit on the route at 6+6 months, and a named Mumbai agent who advertises exactly the extension-plus-supervised-warehousing service you need — but a live contradiction about whether you may leave at all).

**Never.** China and Turkmenistan (no mechanism exists). Tajikistan (15 days, extension requires you in person). Vietnam (45 days, escorted). Iran (see below). Cambodia (no fine cap found, and documented cases of bikes stored with local companies simply vanishing). Kosovo, Montenegro. And **not inside the EU generally** unless you are using the Greek mechanism, because the six-month EU-wide clock runs while you are away and the theoretical exposure is 6% duty plus 17–27% VAT (value added tax) plus seizure risk.

| 32 | **Greece** | Yes, and it is the only purpose-built door in the EU | Under six months, park it and fly with no procedure at all: the tax authority states in terms that immobilisation is not compulsory. | 34 | **Bosnia and Herzegovina** | Yes, de facto | Entry is an oral declaration with nothing placed in your hands, and the allowance is six months in twelve with the clock running while parked. | 35 | **Montenegro** | Avoid | It works today, but a Foreign Vehicle Register is coming, 24-hour pre-notification is being introduced, and fines run €700 to €4,000. **Two things to carry out of this table.** First, in the great majority of these countries what stops you is not the garage, the security or the insurance, it is that **the permit keeps draining while you are away** — so the real instruction for most of them is "be back before it runs out." Second, in every country where you do park it, do the one free thing that converts silence into permission: **a short written notification to the customs temporary-imports unit, naming the storage address and your return date, sent before you fly.** It is the one piece of tradecraft in this report that works everywhere, it is compulsory in Turkey and advised in Pakistan, and it costs you an afternoon.

## Part 3 — The 2026 route reality check

Before the country detail, something you need to know, because it changes the shape of the trip more than any parking rule: **the classic Sydney-to-London overland line is substantially broken right now.** Five separate closures, none of them related to each other, have combined to sever the route in several places.

**Myanmar is closed.** Land borders shut to private foreign vehicles amid the civil war. The US State Department has Burma at Level 4 Do Not Travel (issued 8 May 2026). The Mae Sot–Myawaddy crossing reopened on 28 May 2026 after ten months, but as a cargo trial run with no tourist-vehicle provision, and the precedent from the previous reopening was foreigners permitted only a "border run" with immediate return. Most telling: Big Bike Tours, the operator that pioneered Thailand–Myanmar motorcycle crossings, lists **no tours planned for either 2026 or 2027**. *(MEDIUM-HIGH)*

**The India–Pakistan border at Wagah/Attari is closed.** Shut following the Pahalgam attack of 22 April 2025, still shut as of the most recent reporting found, with no reopening date announced. Limited Afghan cargo trucking resumed; travellers and private vehicles did not. *(MEDIUM-HIGH)*

Those two together mean **India is a cul-de-sac.** You cannot ride into it from the east and you cannot ride out of it to the west. Nepal, Bhutan and Bangladesh are all in-and-back-out via India. Any India leg needs two freight movements, and the 12-month carnet clock should not be spent waiting for Wagah to reopen.

**Iran is at war.** The research found: US and Israeli strikes from 28 February 2026; Ali Khamenei killed and succeeded by his son Mojtaba; the Strait of Hormuz declared closed by the IRGC (Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps) Navy; a Pakistan-brokered ceasefire on 8 April that collapsed on 7–9 July; 300+ US strikes since 10 July; a US naval blockade reimposed 14 July; the Tehran flight information region (FIR) under an EASA (European Union Aviation Safety Agency) "do not operate at any altitude" advisory as of July 2026. DFAT rates Iran **Do Not Travel** with explicit risk of arbitrary detention of Australians, and **the Australian Embassy in Tehran has suspended operations.** Separately, visa rules effective August–September 2025 reportedly require Australian nationals to travel with a licensed guide throughout. *(HIGH on the conflict; MEDIUM on the guide requirement)*

Treat Iran as unavailable. And note the specific reason it is worse than merely dangerous for *your* trip style: parking an asset in a country under naval blockade, where foreign cards do not work so you cannot pay a storage bill remotely, and which you may be unable to re-enter, is the worst possible place to leave a motorcycle.

**Azerbaijan will not let you in.** Foreigners cannot enter by land **or sea** — air only — a restriction in place since March 2020 and extended six consecutive times, most recently to 1 July 2026. You *can* exit by land or sea. This asymmetry kills the westbound Caspian ferry: you cannot ride onto a boat at Kuryk and land at Alat. Journal of Nomads confirmed in February 2026: "Taking the ferry from Baku to Aktau is possible but not the other way around." *(HIGH)*

**Thailand–Cambodia is closed**, per Mad or Nomad's 2026 update — "crossing between Thailand and Cambodia is impossible" — leaving Stung Treng from Laos as the only viable Cambodian motorcycle entry. *(HIGH)*

**Russia is the only remaining rideable westward link, and it is hostile.** Australians are **not eligible for the Russian e-visa** and need a consular sticker visa with an invitation, in person, 2–3 weeks. DFAT rates Russia Do Not Travel with explicit arbitrary-detention risk. Visa, Mastercard and Amex are suspended and foreign-issued cards do not work at Russian merchants or ATMs, so an entire Russian transit must be carried in cash. And Russia is the only land route to Mongolia that does not involve a second China permit. *(MEDIUM-HIGH)*

### What this means for the route

Put together, there are three honest options for getting the bike from Asia to Europe in 2026–27:

**Option A — China transit, then Central Asia, then accept Russia.** Ride SE Asia to the Laos–China border, buy an escorted China transit (US$1,100–2,700 in a scheduled convoy, US$4,000–7,000 solo), exit at Khorgos, Irkeshtam or Torugart into Central Asia, then Kazakhstan into Russia and out at Upper Lars to Georgia. The China segment is a hard, immovable, unpausable 16–40 day window booked six months ahead, and Russia carries the visa, cash and advisory problems above.

**Option B — China transit, then freight the bike over the Caucasus gap.** Same as A, but instead of Russia, ship or truck the bike from Central Asia to Georgia or Turkey and fly yourself. There is documented precedent for the last leg of this: a Georgia-based operator ("Rory", gavshipping@gmail.com) trucks motorcycles Tbilisi–Baku across the closed border, confirmed by a HUBB poster in June 2025. Costs for Central Asia to Georgia air freight: **UNKNOWN**, and this is a significant hole.

**Option C — skip the middle entirely.** Freight the bike from Bangkok or Kuala Lumpur directly to Georgia, Turkey or Greece, and ride Europe. Least romantic, cheapest in aggravation, and — given that your trip design is built around flying home repeatedly — arguably the most compatible with what you actually want, because Georgia and Greece are the two best parking countries on earth for this and you would reach them years earlier.

**My reading:** if China is a bucket-list item, buy it as a **scheduled convoy** rather than a solo permit, and slot it between two flights home rather than trying to pause inside it. If it is not, the Chennai–Bangkok sea freight at a documented **USD 2,330 all-in (2025)** is *cheaper than a solo China permit* and buys unlimited schedule freedom. Note the irony worth sitting with: China is the one country on the route where your entire trip style is impossible, and it sits directly across the middle of the map.

## Part 4 — Country by country

### 1. Australia — getting out

No export approval and no deregistration needed. **The bike must remain Australian-registered for the whole carnet period** — RAC WA (the Royal Automobile Club of Western Australia) states that failure to maintain registration invalidates the carnet. An Export Declaration (EDN) is required because a shipped motorcycle exceeds the AUD 2,000 threshold; your freight agent lodges it. *(HIGH)*

Given your chosen first leg, the departure port is **Darwin, not Sydney**. There is no roll-on/roll-off (ro-ro) passenger ferry to Dili; it is commercial breakbulk or container. Swire Shipping runs the live fortnightly Singapore–Dili–Darwin–Surabaya rotation and announced rate increases effective 25 June 2026, so the service is running inside your window. Historic rider-paid figures: AUD 925 (2015), AUD 900–1,200 (2017), AUD 1,600 (2022), plus USD 70–250 in Dili charges. **2024–26 motorcycle rates: UNKNOWN.** One useful tip from the HUBB: tell them you will load and strap the bike yourself, which reportedly saves about USD 500. *(MEDIUM on operators, LOW on pricing)*

### 2. Australia — getting back in, years later

Three independent gates, and this is where money is lost.

**Duty: free, almost certainly.** Customs Tariff Schedule 4 **item 17** covers "goods that have been exported from Australia and returned to Australia without having been subject to any treatment, repair, renovation, alteration or any other process since their export," at a Free rate. The Australian Border Force's own guidance uses a motor vehicle as its worked example: repair and maintenance necessary for continued safe operation is permitted, but a vehicle with a stereo installed is not, "as this would enhance the value." **So tyres, chains and a rebuilt top end are fine; a big-bore kit, auxiliary tanks or a rack of new luggage bolted on in Kathmandu are arguably not.** Keep the export declaration, pre-departure photographs, odometer records and registration papers as your "same goods, unaltered" evidence pack. *(HIGH)*

**GST (goods and services tax): unresolved, and it is the single largest unquantified exposure at roughly AUD 1,500–2,000.** Schedule 4 item 17 gives duty relief but does **not** appear in the Australian Taxation Office's (ATO) list of Schedule 4 items that make an importation non-taxable. Two shipping brokers assert that motorcycles returning "with proof of previous Australian registration" are GST-exempt. **Get a written ABF or ATO ruling before you ship out.** *(LOW)*

**Import approval: mandatory, one way or the other.** An ABF fact sheet states that vehicles previously exported may be imported using an Australian carnet **if still valid**, and that if the carnet has expired a Vehicle Import Approval becomes necessary — now the **Reimportation Import Approval**, applied for in ROVER *before* import, requiring that you own the vehicle and that **the vehicle is outside Australia** at application. Processing is stated as 30 business days on one departmental page and 60 on another. Importing without approval is an offence. *(HIGH that one is required; MEDIUM on the carnet route, whose best source is pre-2015)*

**This creates a real sequencing constraint nobody mentions.** On a two- or three-year trip you will be on your second or third carnet. The last one must both cover your final carnet countries *and* still be alive when the bike ships home — otherwise you are in a 30-to-60-business-day ROVER queue from abroad. Plan the final carnet's issue date against the homeward shipping date deliberately.

**Biosecurity: the horror-story generator.** All used motorcycles get a full external and internal inspection on arrival unless pre-processed offshore by a DAFF-recognised provider. The standard is free of live insects, seeds, soil, mud, clay, animal faeces and plant material, inspected at wheels, guards, engine bay, underside, chassis rails, vents, under-seat storage, air filter, chain and sprocket. Failure means cleaning at your cost; heavy contamination means export or destruction at your expense. Indicative costs: DAFF inspection AUD 95–145, cleaning 250–450, mould treatment 150–300, underbody deep clean 200–400. One 2018 rider into Darwin spent **three days cleaning in Dili** and then reported "I passed the inspection on the first try… not as hard as all the horror stories told me." Another spent about fifteen hours over three days partially disassembling a BMW GS. *(HIGH on the standard, MEDIUM on costs)*

**Asbestos** import is prohibited outright and ABF may require testing at your expense, with attention to brake components and gaskets on older vehicles. For a modern bike this is a declaration — but **any aftermarket brake pads or gaskets fitted in Pakistan or India are a genuine exposure.** *(MEDIUM)*

### 3. Australia — keeping NSW registration alive

**This is solvable, and the official pathway is better than the forum folklore.** NSW publishes an explicit overseas-renewal process. You must remain a NSW resident temporarily absent. The inspection may be done overseas but the report must come from **"a government department, or an organisation authorised by a government to inspect vehicles,"** must specifically address braking, lighting and steering, and is **valid only 42 days from issue.** A 12-month CTP (compulsory third-party insurance) greenslip from a NSW provider is mandatory. Renew online, or post/email to Service NSW at Parkes. *(HIGH)*

Two operational consequences. The 42-day window means you must time the inspection tightly to the renewal date — you cannot get a roadworthy in Osh in May for an August renewal. And the "government department or government-authorised organisation" filter points you towards countries with a formal periodic inspection regime: **Malaysia's PUSPAKOM** is a government-appointed vehicle inspection company, and Thailand's DLT and Georgia's mandatory technical inspection are government schemes. A bike parked in Malaysia or Georgia is far easier to keep NSW-registered than one in Mongolia or Kyrgyzstan. *(The NSW rule is HIGH; the mapping onto specific foreign inspectorates is inference — get it in writing from Service NSW.)*

**Do not let the registration lapse**, and not only for the obvious reason. EU temporary admission relief under Article 212 requires the bike to be registered outside the EU; if NSW rego dies, the customs basis for relief evaporates simultaneously in every EU country. Your carnet also records the registration details, and a mismatch between carnet and vehicle turns a border into a week. *(HIGH)*

**Other states: UNKNOWN.** Only NSW was verified.

### 4. Timor-Leste

Timor-Leste Customs publishes a dedicated *Temporary Importation of Vehicles* booklet built around the carnet. The carnet is **accepted and is the intended mechanism**, and no financial guarantee is required because "the Carnet replaces the need for you to provide a bond." At entry an electronic **T1C** transit document is created in ASYCUDAWorld (the UN's Automated System for Customs Data) and closed on exit. Processing at Tibar Bay Port (12 km west of Dili) by sea, or Batugade by land — open 09:00–17:00 daily including weekends, X-ray and weighbridge, clearing about 33 cars a day, 30 minutes to several hours. *(HIGH, official)*

**Duration: no numeric limit stated.** Only that "your Carnet is valid for the period you expect to remain in Timor-Leste with your vehicles." No extension procedure is described and **nothing in the booklet requires the owner to remain with or depart with the vehicle.** So the effective cap is your carnet's own twelve months. *(MEDIUM)*

**Can you park it and fly out? UNKNOWN — no firsthand report exists.** The T1C simply stays open until the bike exits, there is no presence rule, and Dili has an established overlander scene (East Timor Backpackers is repeatedly named as the bike-prep hub). The safe play is a written notification to Timor-Leste Customs naming the storage address and return date before you fly.

**One important cross-check:** a 2017 rider's summary of the carnet is the line to remember — "though technically not required for Timor Leste, it was essential for Indonesia and reimporting to Australia."

**Visa trap:** there is **no visa on arrival at the Timor-Leste land border.** You need a Visa Application Authorization arranged in advance (~10 working days), and **the VAA names the specific crossing point** — present it at any other and it is refused. Since you will arrive by sea or air from Darwin, get the VAA for that entry point. *(HIGH)*

### 5. Indonesia — the biggest paperwork risk on the route

**The carnet is absolutely required and there is no way around it.** Mad or Nomad (17 July 2025): "A carnet (CDP) is required to enter Indonesia with a foreign registered motorcycle. There's no way around it other than using one." No source, forum post or ride report describes anyone getting a foreign bike into Indonesia without one. *(HIGH)*

The IMI question is resolved: Ministry of Finance legislation effective **June 2015 transferred all carnet authority from Ikatan Motor Indonesia to Bea Cukai (customs)**, and by 2018 riders confirmed "not a single person asked about or mentioned the IMI." FIA's own advisory still says otherwise and is stale. Email IMI anyway as free insurance; don't wait for a reply. *(MEDIUM-HIGH)*

**A 2026 re-check sharpens this, and explains why the contradiction keeps reappearing.** The governing instrument is **PMK 228/PMK.04/2014**, effective **17 February 2015**, and it is still the live regulation, carried on the Finance Ministry's own legal database (jdih.kemenkeu.go.id/dok/228-pmk-04-2014) and the audit board's (peraturan.bpk.go.id, record 149589). Under it, IMI is Indonesia's appointed **issuing and guaranteeing** body for CPD carnets, exactly as the Chamber of Commerce and Industry (Kadin) is for ATA carnets. But that is a role for *outbound Indonesian* vehicles: an Indonesian applicant must be a current IMI member, submit STNK and BPKB copies, engine and chassis photographs and a full itinerary, pass a physical inspection by IMI, and lodge a refundable deposit of **25% of the vehicle's value**. None of that is a gate on *your* foreign-issued carnet, which Bea Cukai clears directly. **So both camps are right and are talking past each other: IMI issues Indonesian carnets, Bea Cukai clears foreign ones.** Every guide that tells you an IMI letter is required is describing the outbound Indonesian process, not yours. *(MEDIUM-HIGH)*

One procedural detail worth knowing, because it sets the pace at which a claim would reach you: where a carnet vehicle is not re-exported, customs pursues the **guaranteeing organisation** on a **five-working-day** clock. The sources do not make clear whether that is the window in which customs must raise the demand or the window in which the guarantor must pay, and the distinction matters only if something goes wrong. *(MEDIUM on the mechanism, LOW on which reading is right)*

**Now the trap, and it is the sharpest single item in this report.** Indonesia has **two competing legal frameworks** for a foreign private vehicle:

- **PMK 228/PMK.04/2014 — the carnet regime.** Carnet accepted as the customs declaration, duty and import tax fully exempt, **maximum 12 months, one renewal of up to 12 months, 24 months total, nationwide validity.**
- **PMK 52/PMK.04/2019 with PER-5/BC/2021 (a Directorate General of Customs and Excise regulation) — the PPLB land-border regime.** A "Vehicle Declaration," not a carnet. **Maximum 30 days per entry, extendable but not exceeding 6 months per calendar year, and the vehicle may only be operated in the province containing the entry border post.** Permitted only from Malaysia/Brunei into Kalimantan, **Timor-Leste into Nusa Tenggara Timur**, and Papua New Guinea (PNG) into Papua. It even requires the vehicle to arrive with at least three-quarters of a tank of fuel.

If Mota'ain customs default-processes you under PMK 52/2019 — the regulation written specifically for *their* border post — **your bike is legally confined to NTT province for 30 days**, which ends the trip. You must insist on carnet clearance under PMK 228/2014. Whether the officers at Mota'ain have ever done that is **UNKNOWN**. *(MEDIUM-HIGH that both regimes exist and say this)*

**Mitigation, and please take this seriously:** get written confirmation from Bea Cukai in advance naming Mota'ain and your carnet, print PMK 228/2014, carry a Bahasa summary, and hold a **Plan B of shipping Darwin to Surabaya or Jakarta**, where port customs handle carnets routinely (Surabaya and Denpasar are both documented carnet-processing points at around AUD 80 in fees).

**A named Indonesian agent, which the earlier research did not have.** **Naval Cargo**, Bali (+62-8123803813, thrisna@navalcargo.com, weekdays 08:30—17:00) publishes a motorcycle import and export guide for Indonesia built specifically around the CPD carnet, and its document checklist is a useful cross-check on what Bea Cukai actually asks for: the original carnet, valid and issued by a recognised AIT/FIA affiliate; passport copy; visa; boarding pass and e-ticket; a power of attorney if an agent acts for you; **a statement letter setting out your intent and the duration**; the telex-release bill of lading or air waybill; and high-resolution photographs of the bike front, side, and engine and chassis numbers. Their one emphatic warning is the one worth carrying: **make the engine and chassis numbers on the bike match the paperwork exactly, because a single wrong digit gets the vehicle impounded.** This is the agent to put the Mota'ain question to, and the contact to hold if you fall back on shipping into Surabaya or Denpasar. *(MEDIUM—a commercial guide rather than customs, and it says nothing about leaving the bike behind)*

**Can you park it and fly out?** No source addresses it. The one Expat Indo member who asked this exact question got no answer. Reasoning from what is established: the carnet is a customs instrument held against Bea Cukai, **immigration never sees it**, nothing suggests Indonesia endorses vehicles into passports, and the importation voucher simply stays open — which is the correct state while it is parked. Your visa forces the exits anyway (visa on arrival (VOA) ~USD 25 for 30 days, extendable once to 60), so leaving and returning is structurally normal here. On a mid-size adventure bike that is a five-figure AUD exposure. **Set a hard calendar alarm 90 days before carnet expiry.** *(MEDIUM)*

### 6. Malaysia — your staging country

The carnet is not needed at peninsular land borders but **is needed to ship in, and agents want it to ship out** — local advice is that the problem arises when you try to load the bike onto a boat without one. The document is the **ICP** (International Circulation Permit, form JPJK9, **free** from JPJ, the Road Transport Department), requiring foreign registration in Roman letters, a registration certificate with translation, **third-party insurance from a Malaysian provider**, and a licence with translation. **Validity 90 days, extendable with State JPJ Director approval for a further 90, "which does not exceed 180 days in one year."** Insurance via AAM (Automobile Association of Malaysia) at roughly USD 100 per month, although the one firsthand figure describes that USD 100 as covering **the ICP and the insurance together** for one month, and since the ICP itself is free from JPJ, the USD 100 is essentially insurance plus AAM handling; Mad or Nomad calls it "compulsory" and "very difficult," so go through AAM. *(HIGH on the official rules)*

### 7. Singapore — skip it

Entry for a non-Malaysian, non-Thai foreign motorcycle requires an AIT carnet or an ATA (Admission Temporaire/Temporary Admission) carnet, an ICP or Visitor's Permit applied for before entry, a **Singapore-based** third-party insurance policy, an Autopass card, and — because non-Malaysian vehicles cannot self-register — a request submitted via the Land Transport Authority (LTA) online feedback form **"at least a month before" entry**. The VEP for Social Visit Pass holders runs **up to 14 days**. *(HIGH)*

It also gets materially more expensive exactly in your window: from **1 January 2027 the motorcycle VEP rises from S$4 to S$7 (Singapore dollars) per day and the annual ten free VEP days are abolished**, plus a flat S$3 per operational day for vehicles without an ERP2 (second-generation Electronic Road Pricing) On-Board Unit. And Singapore is the one country here whose systems would actively notice a bike sitting inside with no exit transaction, because VEP accrues per day against the plate and settles at the exit gantry. *(HIGH)*

Leaving the bike is effectively prohibited — long-term stay requires full import and registration, which for a motorcycle means a COE (Certificate of Entitlement) and an ARF (Additional Registration Fee) and is economically absurd.

**Verdict: park in Johor Bahru and cross as a pedestrian.** Riding in is a dead end, needs a month's lead time, burns two carnet page-pairs, and caps you at fourteen days.

### 8. Thailand — the best-evidenced answer in Asia

**The carnet is not recognised.** AIT/FIA has no issuing organisation in Thailand; officials may stamp a carnet as a courtesy but Thailand is reportedly clamping down on carnet entries. One April 2026 commercial page claims Thailand *requires* a carnet at a 400% bond — it contradicts every other source and reads as generated filler. Ignore it. *(HIGH)*

Thailand runs a **two-document system** since about July 2016 and you need both:

- **The DLT Foreign Vehicle Permit.** Mandatory for vehicles from countries with no road transport agreement — only Malaysia, Singapore and Laos are exempt. Official: **maximum 30 days per request, annual cap 60 days per calendar year**, 500 baht tourist permit plus 5 baht application plus a 200 baht motorcycle "usage mark," via fvp.dlt.go.th, pre-approval 30 days ahead. *(HIGH)*
- **The customs temporary import.** The officer completes a "simplified customs declaration form" and gives it to you as evidence of temporary import; failure to re-export attracts a 1,000 baht fine per violation. Free at the border. *(HIGH)*

**These two clocks behave completely differently, and the contradiction is unresolved.** The DLT hard-caps at 60 days a year. But the customs paper is reported as extendable, free, 30 days at a time, at *any* Thai customs house, for as long as your visa is valid — and recent HUBB posters confirm obtaining free extensions in Bangkok and Nong Khai. **Confirm this directly with a Thai customs house; it is the most important single thing to nail down for Thailand.**

**Can you park it and fly out? Yes, and the account is remarkable.** Covid caught one couple in Thailand; they waited five months, then "didn't have a choice but to leave our bike in Thailand and fly home to the UK." The bike sat in **Pran Buri with a friend for two years**, with the TIP extended **five times**. On return an official first threatened confiscation plus a one-million-baht fine (the TIP itself states a maximum of 10,000 THB), then said that if they could show they had left Thailand before the final extension expired he would charge 1,000 THB. **They paid 1,000 THB — about AUD 45 — on a two-year-expired TIP.** A friend had paid the full 10,000 THB a month earlier for the same overstay. *(HIGH)*

**The mechanism, plainly: there is no bonded-warehouse route for private overlander bikes. People extend the TIP while in-country, then overstay and pay on exit. The penalty is 1,000 THB per day capped at 10,000 THB.** The cap is the crucial feature — **your worst case is about AUD 450 regardless of how long the bike sits.**

**One serious caveat.** Mad or Nomad's Foreign Vehicle Permit (FVP) article warns that overstaying risks being "red-flagged, preventing future vehicle entry," and specifically that exiting Thailand after the permit expired with the bike still inside risks a permanent bar on bringing a vehicle in again. That is a permanent consequence for a temporary convenience, and it is the worst downside documented anywhere in this research. *(MEDIUM — real but unquantified, and it sits awkwardly beside the couple who did exactly this and rode away for 1,000 baht)*

### 9. Laos

Not a carnet country. A green temporary import form is issued at the border (~60,000 kip, the Lao currency) and must be surrendered on exit. Third-party insurance mandatory, and Lao insurers reportedly will not pay out on a non-Lao licence, so carry the IDP (International Driving Permit). **Can you park it and fly out?** The single best data point is your exact scenario. ### 10. Cambodia

Not a carnet country. ### 11. Vietnam — legal since 2024, practically prohibitive

This is the biggest change since older ride reports, and it cuts both ways. Pre-2024, foreign-registered bikes were effectively banned — a 2008 circular limited temporary import to categories that excluded tourists, and the HUBB records eight riders on Thai-registered scooters **rejected five times at different borders** in 2006.

**Current law: Decree 30/2024/ND-CP, dated 7 March 2024, effective 1 May 2024.** Entry "shall be handled via an international tour operator of Vietnam"; **a lead/escort vehicle is mandatory** ("it is required to have vehicles running ahead to provide traffic guidance for foreign motor vehicles"); **maximum 45 days**, extendable only for force majeure by no more than 10 days; approval by the Ministry of Public Security within 5 working days of a complete dossier; six documents carried at all times including civil liability insurance valid in Vietnam. Two-wheeled motorcycles are eligible. **The carnet plays no part.** *(HIGH)*

Operationally: "solo travel is prohibitively expensive," and for bikes over 175cc a full booked tour with guide, insurance and hotels is required, with permits taking up to 25 working days of operator preparation. **Cost: UNKNOWN reliably** — by analogy with documented escort pricing elsewhere, a two-to-four-week escorted movement is plausibly four figures USD.

**Do riders skip Vietnam? Yes.** Mad or Nomad could not process their own bikes for Vietnam or Cambodia and **rented locally at about USD 50 each** instead. That is the normal answer. *(MEDIUM-HIGH)*

**Parking the bike mid-Vietnam: effectively impossible.** Non-extendable 45-day clock, escorted movement, and the operator carries legal responsibility for the vehicle.

### 13. China — the crux, and a hard no for your trip style

**The carnet is worthless here.** AIT/FIA has no organisation in China; GlobeBusters states flatly that the CPD is "not recognised." *(HIGH)*

Every 2024–26 source agrees on the same package, and it is not negotiable: **a licensed Chinese travel agency is mandatory** (no permit on arrival, ever); **a Chinese guide accompanies you for the entire China section**; **temporary Chinese registration plates** issued at the entry port or nearest vehicle administration office; a **provisional Chinese driving permit** (the IDP is explicitly not recognised); a **pre-approved fixed itinerary lodged with and agreed by government departments**, valid only in the named provinces; plus provincial permits, technical inspection, insurance and **a customs deposit or security guarantee posted on your behalf.** A July 2024 firsthand crossing from Kazakhstan lists exactly this and adds that border formalities alone took a full day — five visual inspections, hazmat-suited disinfection, fingerprinting, X-ray. That rider got "a 27-day permit to drive 7,000 kilometres from Kazakhstan to Laos." *(HIGH)*

**Lead time: 50–60 working days minimum, two to three months realistic, six months recommended.** *(HIGH)*

**Cost, best-evidenced first:** €1,100 per person in a shared convoy versus an initial private quote of €25,000 (firsthand, July 2024); €8,088 for three vehicles over 40 days ≈ €2,700 per vehicle (firsthand); US$900–2,000 per vehicle for the Kyrgyzstan–Pakistan Karakoram Highway (KKH) transit (agency list price); **"$1,700 in a group and up to $7,000 solo"** for entry via Thailand/Laos. **Planning figure: US$4,000–7,000 solo, US$1,100–2,700 in a convoy.** *(MEDIUM)*

Named agencies with evidence found: Ride China, NAVO Tour, Road Pioneer, Tibet Moto, Drive China, China Road Trips, CrissCrossChina, Bike Tour Asia, plus Weitu, ChinaExped and West China Expeditions. **No evidence** that WildChina or Panda Bikers handle own-vehicle transit.

**Can you leave the bike in China? No. Structurally impossible.** The most direct statement, from an operator that actually runs these permits: **"Vehicle must exit China (cannot be left behind or stored permanently)"**, and "all vehicles must be declared, even non-operational ones." Four independent reasons, each fatal on its own: the permit is a transit instrument naming entry port, route, provinces, dates and exit port with no concept of "pause"; the guide's presence *is* the compliance mechanism; an agency has posted a customs security guarantee against your timely exit and will not let a client walk away from a bike on temporary Chinese plates; and extension "requires leaving and re-entering." **No rider has ever been found to have stored a bike in China. No bonded-warehouse route for personal vehicles was found. What China does when a permit expires with the vehicle inside is undocumented anywhere.** *(HIGH on the prohibition; the absence of any storage precedent is a meaningful negative finding in a well-documented community)*

**Practical instruction: treat China as a single continuous sprint of 16–40 days with hard start and finish dates, booked six months out, and schedule every flight home either before you enter or after you exit. China is the one segment of this trip that cannot flex around your flights — plan everything else backwards from the permit dates.**

### 14. Mongolia — easy, and probably not worth it

**No carnet.** At the border customs creates a record against the vehicle and your passport number and issues **a slip of paper** you keep and surrender on exit; there is no passport stamp for the bike. Insurance mandatory, bought at the border, about USD 15–20 for 30 days. Crossing about two hours. *(HIGH)*

**Duration has two layers that disagree.** The Customs Law of Mongolia Article 100.1 sets temporary admission at "not more than one year," and Article 100.2 allows the Customs Central Body to extend "by up to six months for one time." But practice reports say the slip is written against your immigration permission. **Which governs at the border in 2026 is unresolved — ask, and get it in writing.** *(MEDIUM on the law, LOW-MEDIUM on the practice)*

**Can you park it and fly out? Yes, and this is genuinely the easiest country on the route for it.** The informal route is Oasis Café & Guesthouse in Ulaanbaatar — "the meeting point in Mongolia for overlanders," with "the big yard which is handy for all the motorbikes," secure parking, repairs permitted, and **its own carpentry workshop producing motorcycle transport crates**. Direct evidence that exit is unenforced: a rider who disposed of a bike in Ulaanbaatar reported that on departure **"nobody stopped me at the airport or asked for any papers."** The formal route is a customs bonded warehouse — Article 144.1 permits entry and Article 150.1.1 allows **storage up to two years, extendable by one.** *(MEDIUM overall; note the Oasis reviews retrieved are 2010–2011, so current operation is unverified, and the airport account is 2015 and describes a sale)*

**Consequence of the slip expiring: financial rather than criminal.** "If you do not leave with your car you may also have to pay an import tax either on departure or at a later date." Permanent import runs 5% duty plus excise plus 10% VAT, with firsthand reports putting the effective motorcycle assessment at 10–17% of value. *(MEDIUM)*

**The problem is access, not parking.** Mongolia borders **only Russia and China**. Enter from China and leave to Russia and you pay the full China package *plus* take on Russia. Enter and exit via China and you pay **two** China permits — realistically US$8,000–14,000 solo — for a country you could later visit on a fly-in, rent-a-bike basis for a fraction of that. **My recommendation: drop Mongolia from the Sydney–London ride, or accept Russia.** And note the bitter irony: Mongolia is the one country where you could freely park the bike and fly home, and the cost of getting the bike there makes it the worst place on the route to be idle. *(MEDIUM-HIGH)*

### 15. Nepal — and a brand-new system nobody has tested

**Officially a carnet country, routinely waived.** Nepal Tourism Board states plainly: "Overland tourists entering Nepal with their vehicles must possess an international carnet." *(MEDIUM-HIGH)*

**The Bhansar trap is resolved: Bhansar is for Indian-registered vehicles only** (NPR (Nepalese rupee) 150/day motorcycle, 14 days per entry, 30 days per calendar year, requiring an Indian RC (registration certificate) and Aadhaar identity number). It does not apply to you. *(HIGH)*

For a third-country bike there is now a **new digital system**: Nepal's Department of Customs launched **Temporary Import of Vehicle (TIV)**, inaugurated **7 May 2026**. It covers "foreign-registered vehicles" with no geographic restriction, issues a QR code, requires only the bill-book front page and driving licence, has **no carnet field at all**, and is capped at **30 days per fiscal year**. *(HIGH that TIV exists and those are its terms)*

**The 2026 re-check adds four things.** The launch was by the Finance Minister, Dr Swarnim Wagle, at a Kathmandu event on **5 May 2026**, with the system publicly operational from **6 May**; inauguration and go-live are reported variously as 5, 6 and 7 May, so treat it as the first week of May 2026 and stop worrying about the day. It is run by the **Department of Customs under the Ministry of Finance**. It explicitly covers vehicles arriving from **India, Bangladesh and third countries** by land, and the Department confirmed that the service is available for the Chinese border too, merely noting that tourists do not currently arrive that way in Chinese vehicles. And fees are payable online or at designated bank counters, so a TIV entry needs no cash at the border. *(HIGH)*

**Now the single most interesting thing this research turned up anywhere, for your central question rather than for Nepal.** The launch reporting is consistent that where a tourist previously had to present themselves at a customs office, or pay heavy fines, to renew an expired vehicle permit, **the extension can now be filed online**, one account describing it as extending the vehicle's stay "from anywhere within Nepal." **Read the limitation honestly: the wording says within Nepal, not from abroad, and nobody has published an extension filed from another country.** But this is the first temporary-import regime found anywhere on the route with an online extension path at all, and the second unknown in Part 7, whether a permit can be renewed while you are away, finally has a plausible candidate. A QR-code permit, digital payment and a web form are structurally capable of being used from Sydney. **This is worth an email to the Department of Customs before anything else on the Nepal list.** *(MEDIUM that online extension exists; UNKNOWN whether it works from outside Nepal)*

**So Nepal has two doors and they contradict each other.** A carnet reportedly gives **six months within any twelve**; TIV gives **30 days per fiscal year**. Extensions on the non-carnet route are effectively unavailable — only a broken vehicle qualifies, with both garage and police documentation; medical or family reasons do not. Vehicles not removed within six months face confiscation. **Because TIV launched in May 2026, nobody on earth has published a third-country carnet experience under it. This is genuinely open and it determines whether Nepal is a 30-day dash or a six-month base.** *(MEDIUM)*

**Independent confirmation of the carnet door, and a price on the other one.** Nepal's published customs position, restated by the Nepal Tourism Board and by several Nepal-facing operators, is that a personal vehicle brought in by a tourist **under a carnet may stay up to six months within the twelve months from the date of first entry, free of import duty**, while without a carnet the allowance is **30 days in a year and a duty is charged for every day, set by the Finance Act according to the class of vehicle.** So the non-carnet door is not merely shorter, it is metered, which makes the carnet worth carrying into Nepal even though the TIV form has no field for it. The confiscation rule is also blunter than a warning: a vehicle not taken back within six months is stated to be **confiscated ipso facto**, and the only accepted ground for extension remains a broken vehicle, evidenced by both a garage and the police. *(MEDIUM-HIGH — consistent across several independent restatements of the customs rule, but still not read off the Department of Customs' own regulation)*

**Can you park it and fly out? Yes in observed practice.** A dedicated HUBB thread yields the key account: **a rider left a bike in Pokhara for eight months** and reported "the border guards were confused enough at having to stamp a carnet, I don't think they knew about the 6 month restriction." Another reported five-plus months without issue. *(MEDIUM on practice, but the accounts are 2012–2014)*

### 16. India — the longest permit on the route, and a live contradiction

**Carnet required, strictly.** The governing instrument is **Notification No. 296/76-Cus of 2 August 1976**, giving effect to the temporary-importation convention: carnet from an affiliated club, **retention not exceeding six months**, extendable by the Central Government **or the Commissioner of Customs** by a further six months where satisfied it is "necessary in the public interest," and re-export within one year or duty becomes payable. On arrival a **Form X** declaration is filed. The applicant must be normally resident outside India and must not take paid employment. *(HIGH — primary source)*

Each port runs a **centralised Carnet Section** tracking vehicles and monitoring re-export; re-export requires the original export voucher (*volet de sortie*) deposited there. If proof of re-export is not received, **a duty demand is raised on the guaranteeing association within one year of carnet expiry**, with 20% annual interest on overdue claims. The Indian guarantor is the **Federation of Indian Automobile Associations**, Churchgate, Mumbai — administered by the WIAA (Western India Automobile Association) at the same address. A 2011 Team-BHP thread says foreign-issued carnets require **counter-guarantee by FIAA** before use in India; that should be re-confirmed. *(MEDIUM-HIGH)*

**On the 300–400% security multiple: it could not be verified and may be wrong.** Neither AIT/FIA nor FIAA publishes a percentage — both refer only to "a comprehensive formula… based on vehicle's make, model, motor power and final destination." See the carnet section in Part 5, because the Australian answer appears to be very different from what the internet says.

**THE KEY QUESTION, and it is contested.** One specialist source (entryindia.com) states directly that if the tourist travels temporarily the vehicle **"must be deposited into a customs bonded warehouse"**, that a tourist **cannot "leave the country leaving behind his vehicle unattended or with a friend or relative"**, that **"the tourist's passport will be endorsed when a vehicle is cleared"** with the exact re-export date, and that extensions beyond six months are "very rarely given." Against that, a 2011 HUBB poster who entered India overland **three times** said nothing went in his passport and there would be "no hassle about leaving the country without your vehicle."

**Both can be true, and the reconciliation matters for you specifically.** The HUBB poster crossed at **land borders**, where staff barely recognise a carnet and plainly were not endorsing passports. **You will arrive by sea or air freight into Chennai or Mumbai**, which goes through a formal Carnet Section with a Form X declaration and full documentary clearance. **Assume the strict version applies to you.** *(MEDIUM — a secondary source contradicting a fifteen-year-old firsthand account, with no 2020s evidence either way)*

**The other 2011 claim — the "under lock and key" repairs allowance — is CONFIRMED but was materially understated.** The actual rule text reads: *"The vehicle is garaged, except when it is being repaired in a premises approved by the Commissioner of Customs under a double lock, one of the owner and the other of the Customs, provided that where the holder… is hospitalised then this condition shall not apply."* So the concession is real but requires **Commissioner-approved premises with Customs physically holding one of two keys** — a bonded, supervised arrangement, not a friendly workshop. The hospitalisation limb waives the *garaging* condition, not the time limit. Whether Customs would approve double-lock garaging so you could holiday in Australia is **untested and no account of anyone trying was found.** *(HIGH on the text, MEDIUM at best on availability)*

**The single most actionable India lead:** **Seco Shipping** (Mumbai / Nhava Sheva, +91-9892117786, info@secoshipping.com) advertises, for carnet vehicles, customs clearance under carnet at Indian ports, **assistance extending the carnet period from the initial 180 days by a further 180**, and **"warehoused vehicles during extended retention periods with supervision."** That is your use case, described by a named agent. Note the tension: entryindia says extensions are "very rarely given" while Seco sells the service routinely — unresolved. *(MEDIUM)*

**Named storage exists but is legally the wrong shape.** **Stonehead Bikes**, Vivek Vihar, New Delhi (+91-8800371553), an official Royal Enfield rental partner operating since 2009, advertises covered indoor storage with CCTV, trickle charging and monthly inspections **from ₹1,500/month**, and explicitly names **monsoon pauses (June–September)** and visa runs as use cases. **But it requires an "RC photocopy" — the market is built around Indian-registered bikes owned by foreigners, and the page makes no claim to handle carnets or customs documentation.** Parking a carnet vehicle there is precisely the arrangement entryindia says is not permitted. Use it for a two-week visa run; do not build a three-month absence on it. **Goa: UNKNOWN** — searches surfaced only rental operators; the Goa monsoon-storage market is real for Indian-plated bikes but no operator handling a carnet vehicle could be evidenced. *(MEDIUM)*

**Duty exposure if it goes wrong.** Post-Budget-2025 basic customs duty on CBU (completely built up, meaning fully assembled) motorcycles is 40% up to 1600cc and 30% above, and that is BCD alone — add AIDC (Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess), Social Welfare Surcharge, and **IGST (Integrated Goods and Services Tax) at 28% plus compensation cess**, so effective exposure on a seized bike comfortably exceeds the vehicle's value. Add 20% annual interest, and note that non-re-export is treated as a **smuggling offence** with the demand falling on FIAA, which pursues the AAA (the Australian Automobile Association), which pursues you. *(HIGH on rates)*

**An Indian registration workaround does not exist.** Only Indian residents with proof of residence may register vehicles; foreigners may purchase for direct export only; and "under no circumstances may a CPD be issued outside of India for a vehicle purchased in India or bearing Indian registration plates." *(MEDIUM-HIGH)*

### 21–26. Central Asia and Russia

**No carnet needed in any of them** — Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan and Russia all issue a temporary import document at the border. *(HIGH)* But **keep carrying the carnet** for India and Pakistan.

**The EAEU angle is the structural fact.** Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Russia, Belarus and Armenia form a single customs space; a vehicle admitted to one may travel the others without re-importation at internal borders, with a **12-month EAEU-territory ceiling**, and each exit from and return to the union requires a fresh temporary import. Secondary sources put the EAEU rule at "not more than 1 year," extendable by customs to a maximum of two, with extension filed before expiry or no later than one month after. *(MEDIUM)*

**Kyrgyzstan — the reputation is real but out of date, and a 2025 rule change matters.** The community belief that Kyrgyzstan is the great Central Asian parking spot rests almost entirely on 2013–14 HUBB threads — and the Osh operator who gave the most favourable accounts (MuzToo) **himself predicted in August 2014 that EAEU accession would end the informal regime in January 2015.** Kyrgyzstan joined in August 2015. **No post-2015 firsthand account of leaving a bike there and flying out could be found.** *(the favourable accounts are LOW-MEDIUM and dated; the modern practice is UNKNOWN)*

Meanwhile, effective **3 May 2025**, Kyrgyzstan imposed a separate regime on foreign-plated vehicles, registered in the Unified System of External Migration Accounting and synced to MVD (Ministry of Internal Affairs) databases: **a vehicle may remain not more than six months, after which it must leave Kyrgyzstan for at least 30 calendar days before returning**, with liability under Article 38 of the Code of Offenses for breach. A further tranche from **1 October 2025** requires **temporary state registration** of foreign-plated vehicles. *(HIGH — two independent state-media reports plus a third on the October tranche)*

**Read that carefully, because it changes the answer.** The six-month presence cap is a *road and registration* parameter separate from the customs clock, and the shorter of the two binds. A trip home of four or five months inside a single six-month window is comfortably legal. **Anything approaching six months requires the bike to physically leave the country and stay out for 30 days — which cannot happen while you are in Australia.** *(MEDIUM — the rule is HIGH; that parked time counts is the natural reading but is unstated)*

### 30. Turkey — legal, documented, and structurally hostile to your trip

Turkey admits a tourist's foreign-plated vehicle under *geçici ithalat* (temporary import). At the border the bike is **written into your passport** and recorded in the customs system, now queryable via the Ministry of Trade's *Yabancı Plakalı Taşıt İşlemleri* portal at ypti.ticaret.gov.tr, which displays the last date the vehicle may remain. *(HIGH)*

**The 730-day figure is real but does not apply to you.** The Turkish text confines it to Turkish citizens resident abroad, dual nationals and Blue Card holders; foreign nationals *with* a Turkish residence permit also get up to 730 days. For a foreign national **without** a residence permit, the Ministry of Trade's English FAQ is unambiguous: "the time limit granted to vehicles of foreigners is the period of these people can stay in Turkey." Independent restatements put that at **90 days per visit / 180 days within any 180-day period**, and a rider who did it confirms "foreign vehicles are only allowed in Turkey for 3 months out of every year." **Plan on 90 days of bike-clock, not 730.** No source at all supports the "730 days within a 5-year period" formulation — treat that as garbled. *(HIGH)*

Watch the **185-day test** as well: to count as "resident abroad" you must have spent 185+ days outside Turkey in the preceding twelve months. Automatic on first entry, but it means **a quick hop to Bulgaria and back to reset the clock is not reliably available.** *(MEDIUM)*

**Leaving Turkey without the bike — four options, and only one stops the clock.**

**(a) The Ek-7 *taahhütname*.** A *taahhütname* is a formal written undertaking. Customs Law Article 241/6(e) makes prior notification compulsory. File the "Ek-7 Taahhütname Formu" in two signed copies at the nearest customs office, declaring the address where the bike will be parked and that nobody else will use it. An e-Devlet service exists ("Yurt dışına taşıtsız çıkışlarda taahhütname verme/kaldırma") but **whether an Australian tourist holding only a passport can log in is UNKNOWN — assume you must go in person.** Filing is **free**; do it at least a day before flying. **The critical limitation: under a taahhütname the vehicle's clock keeps running** — the Turkish wording is *"taşıtın süresi işlemeye devam eder."* Your time in Australia buys you nothing back. So this lets you exit lawfully and fine-free, but a three-month absence consumes your entire 90-day allowance and you return to a bike that is already illegal. *(HIGH)*

**(b) Surrender to customs supervision / *yediemin otoparkı* — the clock-stopper.** Turkish sources say vehicles left with the customs administration or a trustee impound may remain under customs supervision **for three months from delivery, extendable by a further three: six months maximum.** On the clock, the evidence points to suspension — the Ministry of Foreign Affairs traveller guide states the counter pauses and no penalties accrue, a 2025 guide says "the vehicle's entry record will be temporarily closed in your passport," and the official FAQ's negative phrasing ("in case you go abroad without your vehicle **by not** leaving it to the customs office, there will be no adding to the time") only makes sense if surrendering it *does* stop the clock. **Cost: UNKNOWN — no source gives a tariff.** Downside: you lose physical access and the bike sits in an impound yard. *(MEDIUM — and this is the most important unresolved point in the Turkey section. Get it in writing from a Turkish customs office before relying on it. The English FAQ says one month where the Turkish sources say three, which suggests a stale translation.)*

**(c) Transfer to another person — closed off.** "The transfer of title for the vehicles imported temporarily within the scope of private use is not allowed," with a narrow same-family exception that does not cover a tourist's plain temporary import. *(HIGH)*

**(d) Just fly out and leave it with a friend.** A defined offence with a defined price. Article 241/6(e) failure-to-notify is a **single flat fine: 9,528 TL (Turkish lira) in 2025, 11,952 TL in 2026.** Plus overstay fines because the clock kept running: 2026 rates of 2,988 TL up to one month, 5,976 TL at two, 8,964 TL at three. **Beyond three months' overstay, Article 238 imposes a penalty equal to one quarter of the vehicle's customs duties, minimum 11,952 TL, plus explicit seizure and confiscation risk.** So a two-to-three-month absence costs roughly **15,000–21,000 TL** — meaningful but not ruinous. **The cliff is at three months. Never cross it.** *(HIGH)*

**What actually happens — the one detailed firsthand account.** A rider in 2023 visited customs near Antalya airport two to three days before flying, bringing passport, registration and **a printout of valid Turkish insurance**, filled in a form with personal details, plate, storage address and vehicle entry date, and received a stamped form with a three-month expiry — which she photographed, because no copy was issued. On return she retrieved the bike and **paid the overstay fine at the border on exit: about 1,000 TL per month, settling 1,600 TL for two months after a cash discount.** *(MEDIUM — single account, but detailed and recent)*

**Nobody reported being physically blocked from boarding a flight.** But enforcement tightened in October 2025: customs now runs digital checks that **block a foreign vehicle's departure** until unpaid tolls, bridge fees and traffic fines are settled, even without formal notification. **Clear your HGS balance (Turkey's electronic toll account) before heading for the border.** *(HIGH)*

**Does the carnet help? Almost certainly not.** Turkey does not require a CPD. The document people confuse with it is Turkey's own *Yabancı Taşıtlar Geçici Giriş Karnesi*, issued by TÜRING (the Turkish Touring and Automobile Club) for holders of work, student or retirement residence permits, giving up to two years with Turkish MA–MZ "guest" plates — tourist and family permits do not qualify. **Whether Turkish customs would stamp a foreign FIA/AIT carnet instead of writing the bike into your passport is UNKNOWN and no source describes it being done. Treat "use the carnet to dodge the passport link" as probably wrong.**

**Insurance:** Turkish compulsory third-party is mandatory for entry — "they won't let you past immigration without it" — at around €30–35 for 90 days, and **the policy must be valid for the entire period the vehicle stays**, checked as part of the customs permission. Keep it live while stored. *(MEDIUM)*

### 31. The EU — one clock, and it never stops

**The law.** Union Customs Code (UCC) Articles 250–253 create temporary admission; Delegated Regulation 2015/2446 fills in the detail. **Article 212** requires the vehicle be "registered outside the customs territory of the Union in the name of a person established outside that territory" and "used by a person established outside" it — you satisfy all three limbs cleanly. **Article 217** sets the discharge period for privately used road transport at **six months**, running "from when goods are placed under the procedure." Confirmed independently by German customs ("the standard duration is six months") and Finnish Customs. *(HIGH)*

**One EU-wide clock, not one per country.** Finnish Customs is explicit that "the EU is a single customs territory," so the limits are not national. **Riding France to Germany to Poland resets nothing.** A 2015 HUBB post asserting "no more than 6 months in any one country within EU" is simply wrong; do not plan on it. *(HIGH)*

**There is no "in any twelve months" in EU law** — that phrasing belongs to the UK rule and Greece's national implementation. Since re-export discharges the procedure and re-entry is a fresh declaration by conduct, the textual reading is that **exiting the EU customs territory genuinely resets the six months.** *(MEDIUM — no official source states this in terms, and repeated day-trips to reset would invite a circumvention argument. This is the single highest-value question to put in writing to a national customs authority; German and Dutch customs both answer email.)*

Exits that count: Serbia, Bosnia, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Albania, Kosovo, Turkey, the UK — and **Switzerland**, which is outside the EU customs territory. *(MEDIUM on Switzerland, inferred from the customs-territory definition)*

**Nothing is recorded on entry.** No form, no stamp, no database entry — the vehicle is placed under temporary admission by the act of crossing the border, which is deemed to be the customs declaration. **There is no vehicle-to-passport linkage anywhere in the EU** and no clock any official can look up. A persistent myth that a passport stamp bearing a car symbol records a vehicle is false; it means you entered at a road border. *(HIGH / MEDIUM)*

**Can you leave the bike and fly out? Yes. That breaches nothing — there is no presence requirement anywhere in the EU rules. But the six months keeps burning while you are in Sydney.** *(HIGH)* The three ways to stop it: ride it out; place it in **customs warehousing**, which discharges temporary admission and has **no time limit** (the clean, legal, indefinite answer — **cost UNKNOWN, no provider publishes rates**); or apply for extension under UCC Article 251, which is permitted only "in case of exceptional circumstances," is well-trodden for superyachts and essentially untrodden for motorcycles. **Do not plan on the extension.** *(HIGH on warehousing; MEDIUM-LOW on extension)*

**Can a friend move it?** Carefully. German customs sets out narrow exceptions for EU residents using a non-EU vehicle: occasional use "according to the instructions" of the registered owner. **Storing it in a friend's garage is not "use" and is unproblematic; lending it for a weekend ride is a breach.** *(MEDIUM-HIGH)*

**What actually happens.** The firsthand record is consistent and unflattering to the law — and the closest analogue to you that exists is a rider who kept an **Australian-registered** bike in the **UK from 2011 to 2015**, riding annual Europe trips: on entry "all they wanted was to see the rego paperwork and make sure I had insurance," and on the limit, "whilst in theory you're supposed to remove your bike from the EU within 6 months… it doesn't seem to be policed" — with no consequence even after reporting it stolen at one point. Others report non-EU bikes stored in Ireland and Germany for years, and European garage storage at about €30/month. **But almost all of this is 2012–2016, and Part 1's warning applies with full force.** *(MEDIUM on the accounts, LOW as a forecast)*

**Exposure if caught:** a customs debt of **6% duty on motorcycles** plus **VAT at the member state's rate (19% Germany, 21% Spain/Netherlands, 22% Italy, 24% Greece, 27% Hungary)** on customs value plus freight. On a €10,000 bike, roughly **€2,900–3,500** plus national penalties. Not ruinous — but **the seizure risk matters more than the money, because a confiscated bike ends the trip.** *(MEDIUM)*

**Insurance is a real problem.** Australia is not in the Green Card system. Three verified products: **ADAC** (the German automobile club) third-party liability for foreign-registered motorcycles at about **€22/month**, but you must apply **in person at a German ADAC office** with original registration papers; **Alessie** (Netherlands), two to twelve months, **€288.15 for two months then €88.49 per additional month**, minimum age 25; and **TourInsure** (Hamburg), international motor and frontier insurance for foreign-registered motorcycles, arranged by post before departure. **Whether cover must stay live while stored: UNKNOWN — store strictly off-road in a private garage and the question largely disappears.** *(MEDIUM)*

**Member-state quirks:** Italy gives one year to EU-plated vehicles driven by non-residents and is silent on non-EU plates, so the EU six months governs; Spain applies six consecutive months for non-residents "regardless of the vehicle's origin," with fines €500–1,500 and immobilisation. Both regimes are aimed at *residents* running foreign plates, not tourists. Portugal, France, Netherlands, Germany, Hungary, Croatia and Romania: nothing beyond the EU baseline found.

### 32. Greece — not the trap I expected; the only purpose-built door in the EU

I asked the research team to investigate a suspicion that Greece requires a non-EU vehicle to be sealed if the owner leaves without it. **The opposite is true within the six months, and the sealing regime turns out to be a benefit rather than a penalty.**

**The clock.** AADE: "the duration of temporary importation-use is determined at six (6) months, either continuous or not, per twelve-months," under Ministerial Decision D.247/88 with penalties under Article 137 of Law 2960/01. Note this is a **national six-in-twelve formula, stricter than the EU's plain six months, run as its own clock alongside it.** *(HIGH)*

**The decisive sentence**, from AADE's own FAQ: *"If a person has received a passenger vehicle… and before the expiry of the period… the beneficiary departs from Greece without the vehicle, **immobilization of the vehicle by the customs is not compulsory**."* **So you can park in Athens, fly to Sydney, and come back — no procedure, no seal, no cost — provided you are back and the bike is out before six months.** *(HIGH)*

**At six months, immobilisation becomes available and compulsory** if the bike is not re-exported: vehicles must be immobilised by the competent customs authority **upon request of the beneficiary**, and "remain immobilized for six (6) months the least and twenty four (24) months the most." To release it you must prove you had your normal residence abroad during the last twelve months before unsealing — which as an Australian resident you satisfy trivially. *(HIGH)*

**And since 2023 the procedure is digital and painless.** Requests go through **myaade.gov.gr** ("My requests" → Customs → "Vehicles – Temporary Import" → "Immobilization and mobilization of vehicles"), and — this is the remarkable part — **"immobilization of the vehicle is carried out, under the sole responsibility of the beneficiary, in private parking place, without presence of a customs officer."** Fees paid electronically. *(HIGH — three sources: AADE, National Herald, icsgr)*

**Two frictions.** AADE's main page mentions that "a third-party guarantee of a reliable person has to be submitted to the supervising customs authority amounting to the due customs duty and taxes" — a Greek guarantor standing behind roughly €3,000 is a real problem for a foreigner with no contacts, and the FAQ does not mention it. *(MEDIUM, single source, unresolved.)* And myAADE requires TAXISnet credentials, meaning a Greek tax number, obtainable for non-residents but a day's work.

**Verdict: Greece is the only EU member state with a documented, digital, officially-sanctioned way to legally park a non-EU vehicle for up to 24 months without re-exporting it.** The cost is that immobilisation locks the bike for a **minimum of six months** — you cannot seal it for three weeks and ride again.

**The crackdown is real but aimed elsewhere.** A Finance Ministry bill published 19 June 2026 sets fines by engine size for EU passenger cars (€2,500 to €10,000), €2,000 for failing to submit an arrival declaration, €1,500 for driving past deadline, €700 for unauthorised third-party driving, with on-the-spot immobilisation, removal to state facilities at the owner's cost, and **forfeiture to the state if unclaimed after three months**. A recent operation immobilised **229 foreign-plated luxury vehicles.** The targets are Greeks and residents dodging registration tax on foreign plates, not overlanders — but the machinery now exists. *(HIGH)*

### 33–38. The non-EU Balkans

Five of the six run the **identical six-months-in-twelve** formula (Serbia, Bosnia, Montenegro, North Macedonia); **Kosovo is worse at three months**; **Albania is the outlier at 12 months plus a 12-month extension**. No carnet is needed or useful anywhere. In Serbia, Bosnia, Montenegro and North Macedonia **no document is placed in your hands and no vehicle record is created**, so flying out is administratively invisible — but **no country publishes any deposit, seal or bonded-storage procedure, so there is no legal park-and-fly mechanism either, and each country's clock runs while the bike is parked.** *(HIGH/MEDIUM)*

**Is parking in the Balkans the answer to the EU clock? Partly.** The mechanism works — riding out of the EU stops the EU clock, and each Balkan state gives a separate bucket. But **no country except Albania gives more time than the EU does**, so the gain is additional jurisdictions, not longer stays.

**Ranking: Albania** (12 months, cheapest insurance — SIGAL sells border third-party cover **online** in 15-day to one-year terms at roughly €12.80–22.40 for 15–30 days — and no register) **> Serbia** (best physical storage: Torobox Belgrade offers indoor guarded motorcycle-sized units; best long-haul airport; but brutal insurance at about €121.80/month and fines up to 750,000 RSD (Serbian dinar) with confiscation) **> North Macedonia > Bosnia** (frontier insurance sold at the border only, never online) **> Montenegro — avoid** (incoming Foreign Vehicle Register, 24-hour pre-notification, €700–4,000 fines, and **likely EU accession inside your window, which would sweep a parked bike *into* the EU customs territory**) **> Kosovo — avoid** (three months, entries now recorded, and no Green Card recognised except Albanian and Macedonian).

**One trap worth naming:** the two obvious overlander storage businesses in the region — **MotoCamp Bulgaria** (widely recommended on the HUBB) and **Croatia Moto Adventure** — are both **inside the EU**. Parking there burns the EU clock.

### 39. The UK

**Six months in any twelve** — gov.uk: "you only use the vehicle for up to 6 months in total — this can be a single visit, or several shorter visits over 12 months," with nidirect adding that "the vehicle must keep to the registration and licensing requirements of its home country." Conditions: visiting, not resident, private use, **do not sell, lend or hire**, re-export within six months. *(HIGH)*

**No paperwork required.** The Temporary Admission handbook from HMRC (His Majesty's Revenue and Customs) lists means of transport among goods declarable "by any other act": "you do not need to fill any forms as the act of crossing the border counts as your declaration." Form **C110** exists and gov.uk suggests carrying it; apply weeks ahead **if the bike arrives as freight** rather than ridden. No NOVA (Notification of Vehicle Arrivals), no DVLA (Driver and Vehicle Licensing Agency) notification — those are permanent-import only. *(HIGH)*

**You can leave the bike and fly out** — nothing requires presence. **Do not let anyone else ride it**; "lend" is an express breach. *(HIGH)*

**At six months, three routes:** ride it to France (a separate customs territory post-Brexit, so it discharges UK admission and opens a fresh EU period); apply for extension (citex.temporaryadmissionsteam@hmrc.gov.uk / 0300 322 7064, but "approved in exceptional circumstances… case by case" — the 24-month figure is the generic ceiling, **not an entitlement**); or **customs warehousing**, which HMRC confirms discharges temporary admission and has **no time limit**. **Moss Automotive** (mossauto.co.uk) is a verified HMRC-authorised bonded warehouse thirty minutes from central London that advertises handling temporary import paperwork; **rates UNKNOWN.** *(HIGH)*

**Insurance:** no mainstream UK insurer was found writing cover on foreign plates — UK brokers advertising "imported bike insurance" mean UK-registered grey imports, not foreign-plated vehicles. The ADAC/Alessie/TourInsure continental products should extend to GB but this is **UNVERIFIED and is the highest-value insurance question to settle before you arrive.** SORN (Statutory Off Road Notification) and MOT (the annual roadworthiness test) do not apply to a vehicle with no DVLA record. **Store off-road** and the exposure largely vanishes. *(MEDIUM)*

## Part 5 — The cross-cutting machinery

### The carnet: what it actually costs an Australian

**This is where the internet will mislead you, and the research turned up a direct contradiction that is worth resolving before you spend anything.**

Two of my research teams read the same document — the AAA's Carnet de Passages application form — and reported opposite things. One reported a percentage-of-value security scheme (India 400%, Pakistan 400%, Iran 470%, Egypt 200%) with a 2%-of-value indemnity option. The other reported a **flat structure with no percentages at all**. A third pass to break the tie read the form properly and confirmed: **the 2018 AAA form contains no percentages.**

The structure on that form is:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Application fee | **AUD 820** |
| Insurance premium (Millers, indemnifying the AAA) | **AUD 100** |
| Refundable bond | **AUD 500** |
| **Total** | **AUD 1,420** |

Plus expedite surcharges of AUD 100–200 depending on turnaround, twelve months' validity, and **two years to return the fully discharged original carnet or the bond is void.** *(MEDIUM-HIGH — official AAA document, but dated November 2018; a July 2024 form reportedly exists at aaa.asn.au and 404s. **Current 2026 fees: UNKNOWN. Ring the AAA on +61 2 6247 7311 or email international@aaa.asn.au.**)*

**So where do the 400–470% figures come from?** Almost certainly from other clubs — ADAC bands India, Iran, Pakistan and Sri Lanka together in its highest category (€30,000 security on a €25,000–50,000 vehicle), and one rider was quoted about £4,000 per bike through the RAC (the UK's Royal Automobile Club) for India. Horizons Unlimited independently states that deposits range from 25% (New Zealand) to 470% (Iran) across issuers. **The Australian club appears to be one of the cheapest in the world for exactly the countries that are most expensive elsewhere.** That is a genuine and underappreciated advantage of holding an Australian carnet, and it is worth confirming in writing before you plan around it.

**The critical caveat on the form:** *"The insurance premium does not relieve you of paying duty should the claim arise."* The indemnity protects the AAA, not you. **You remain personally and financially liable for duties, taxes and penalties.** *(HIGH)*

**How a claim actually arises:** an import counterfoil in a country remains unmatched by an export counterfoil → that country's customs raises a duty-and-tax demand → the demand goes to the AAA → the AAA calls on your security → the AAA or the insurer pursues you personally. Customs "may assign a different value than the one supplied on the carnet," so the demand can exceed the declared value. **The AIT/FIA claim-notification deadline — how long after expiry a country can still raise a demand — is UNKNOWN, and it determines how long your liability tail runs after the trip ends.** *(MEDIUM)*

### Carnet expiry while the bike is parked — the trap that costs the most

**Validity is twelve months.** On a 2026–28 trip you will need at least one and probably two renewals from the road. *(HIGH)*

**It can be renewed while you and the bike are abroad, and this is the single most useful mechanical fact in this section.** The AIT/FIA guidance is explicit: for renewal while travelling you **"contact the AIT/FIA appointed CPD-issuing organization in the country where the vehicle is currently in. It will liaise with its Customs authorities."** So the machinery for "my carnet dies while the bike sits in a garage in Tbilisi" exists — the local club acts as your agent with local customs while the AAA authorises. ADAC is documented couriering replacement carnets internationally by DHL; **whether the AAA does the same, and at what cost, is UNKNOWN.** *(HIGH on the mechanism)*

An important distinction: an **extension** is generally endorsed by, and valid only in, the country the vehicle is currently in. To keep crossing borders you need a **follow-up (replacement) carnet** — a fresh twelve-month book, new security, new admin fee. Replacements are reportedly available up to four times after the initial carnet, i.e. five years total. The AAA's published extension fee was **AUD 150 plus postage** (2022 form). *(MEDIUM)*

**Start six weeks before expiry.** The mirror-image Swiss procedure requires application before expiry or within 60 days of it, with local customs endorsement and visa validity both bearing on approval. *(MEDIUM)*

**The document that saves you is the Certificate of Location** — the last page of the carnet, dated and stamped by customs, police or another competent authority in the country where the vehicle physically is. It proves the bike did not vanish into the local market, and it is what unlocks your security. One rider in Finland had a €5,000 bond withheld despite complete entry and exit stamps until a Certificate of Location was produced. **Know that this page exists and what it is for.** *(MEDIUM)*

**And the good news about discharge:** a carnet must generally be discharged with the vehicle in a **non-carnet country** — you are not required to return the motorcycle to Australia, only to have the final page stamped by customs in a non-carnet nation. **Europe and the UK qualify.** A UK customs officer or police station is the target. (That a UK motoring club or a consulate can stamp it is **UNCONFIRMED** — don't rely on it.) *(MEDIUM)*

**Enforcement is uneven, which is a trap rather than a comfort.** One rider reports "my Carnet expired when I was in Africa but at most of the borders nobody realised. They stamped it as usual." An expired carnet that gets stamped anyway leaves you with a document the AAA will not accept as a discharge, and your deposit is the hostage.

**One more piece of arithmetic:** a carnet has a limited number of page-pairs. Your route as sketched burns Timor-Leste in and out, Indonesia in and out, Malaysia in and out, plus any Thailand reset trips, plus India and Pakistan. **Count the pages before ordering and buy the larger booklet.**

### The one procedural trick worth stealing

Buried in a January 2022 HUBB thread about foreign bikes *in* Australia is the best single piece of tradecraft this research produced. A rider contacted Australia's "National Temporary Importations & Securities" unit and **obtained written permission to leave a carnet bike with a friend while he flew to Europe.** The conditions were: notify the departure date, the duration, the storage address, and notify again on return. Another poster in the same thread: customs is "not as draconian as some make out" if you are "honest and up front."

**This is the model to replicate in every country you park in: a short written notification to the customs temporary-imports unit, naming the storage address and your return date, before you fly.** It is free, it takes an afternoon, it is fully consistent with the carnet holder's obligation to notify the issuer if anything prevents re-export, and it converts an ambiguous silence into a documented permission. In Turkey it is literally the law (the Ek-7). In Pakistan it is the one thing the sole source on the subject tells you to do. **Do it everywhere.**

### Insurance for a bike stored abroad

**No insurer covering an Australian-registered bike parked in Asia could be found.** My honest read is that this broadly does not exist as an off-the-shelf product and riders self-insure — but that is inference, not an established negative. *(UNKNOWN)*

**One country makes it a legal condition.** Turkey's park-and-fly procedure requires valid insurance **for the entire period the vehicle remains**, checked as part of the customs permission. So "self-insure" is not universally available. *(HIGH)*

**Read the storage providers' feature lists as your risk checklist**, because they are selling against the real failure modes: ventilated indoor space, bike covers, **battery removal or trickle charging**, **monthly engine start-ups**, **tyre rotation**, and explicitly **climate-controlled low-humidity** space. Chiang Mai Storage's customers report **no mould after three months and none after nearly two years** in climate control. **In South-East Asia, mould — not theft — is the enemy, and your riding gear is more vulnerable than the bike.** HiVolt sells low-humidity *gear* storage as a separate line item for exactly that reason. *(HIGH)*

**Practical preparation:** pay for climate control or at least ventilated indoor space in the tropics; remove the battery or put it on a tender; leave the tank full with stabiliser (a full tank means less internal condensation); use paddock stands or over-inflate against flat-spotting, or pay for monthly rotation; block the exhaust and airbox against rodents; use a breathable cover, not a tarp; pay the small extra for a monthly start-up if offered. **Take the registration papers, carnet and keys with you** — never leave the paperwork with the bike. Photograph the odometer and the whole bike at handover.

**Security beats insurance.** The Cambodian warning that "bikes stored with Khmer companies to disappear" argues for storing with a business whose reputation is visible in the overland community — HiVolt, Bike Tour Asia, AlaaToo, StoreSafe — rather than the cheapest lockup.

### The IDP problem, and why it fits your trip perfectly

**Australian IDPs are valid twelve months, and you cannot renew one from abroad.** NRMA (the National Roads and Motorists' Association): validity is "12 months from the date of issue **or until your Australian driver licence expires**, whichever comes first," and explicitly "you cannot apply for or renew an IDP while abroad." Cost AUD 55. There is a tension in NRMA's own text, which also says IDPs can be mailed internationally — most plausibly the application must originate in Australia but the permit can be posted to you. *(HIGH on validity; MEDIUM on the mailing)*

**For a chunked trip this is a non-problem and actually a scheduling gift.** Every time you fly home, renew the IDP before you fly out again, and make sure your NSW licence expiry sits comfortably beyond the next leg, because the licence expiry caps the IDP. **Never start a leg with less than a leg's worth of IDP.** If a leg looks like running past twelve months, plan the home visit around the IDP rather than the other way around.

One caveat: Australia issues under the 1949 Geneva Convention and several countries on your route are 1968 Vienna parties who occasionally quibble. No firsthand report of this biting a rider was found. Carry your NSW licence with the IDP always.

### Shipping — costs and named agents

Storage costs AUD 30–70 a month; a single air freight costs AUD 4,000–8,000. **Freight is the right answer when it replaces an unrideable leg or converts a carnet problem into a non-carnet country — not when it merely relocates a parked bike.**

Indicative figures, best-evidenced first:

- **Chennai ↔ Bangkok by sea: USD 2,330 all-in** (USD 1,200 shipping + USD 1,130 clearing), firsthand 2025. **The single best-evidenced way to skip both China and Myanmar.**
- **UK/Europe → Australia by sea: Get Routed publishes 2027 prices — A$3,845 one way, A$5,235 return**, for a bike under 2,300 mm, including sea freight and customs clearance both ends, out of **Felixstowe**, A$600 deposit, **minimum ten bikes for a return shipment — book early.** Autoshippers quotes shared-container crated rates **from £483 to Sydney** (a floor). James Cargo: UK→Australia sea USD 1,200–4,000, air USD 2,800–4,200. **Realistic all-in £1,200–2,200.**
- **Air into Bangkok:** USD 3,200–5,800 from Western Europe, ~96-hour transit. Shared 20 ft container into Thailand USD 1,400–2,200 over 35–45 days. RoRo USD 1,100–1,600 over 8–12 weeks. Customs broker USD 180–320 per clearance. **Bonded storage at Laem Chabang ~USD 28/day.**
- **Malaysia 90-day bike storage: USD 145.** Malaysia↔Indonesia about USD 500 each way plus a USD 100 flight.

**Verified agents:** **Get Routed** (getrouted.com.au, 1300 466 686) — Australian, 25+ years, the obvious contact for both the London leg and bringing the bike home. **James Cargo** (jamescargo.com) and **Moto Freight** (motofreight.com) — UK. **Dietrich Logistics Malaysia** (KL) — freight plus warehousing. **Eagle Export / Sun Nepal** (Kathmandu). **Seco Shipping** (Mumbai/JNPT) — carnet specialist. **AlaaToo Moto** (Osh) — workshop plus shipping. **Giga Shipping** (gigaship.com, +60-3-5569 1188, Shah Alam) — Malaysian roll-on/roll-off and customs clearance, Port Klang to Kuching and Kota Kinabalu, the sea leg into Malaysian Borneo.

**Two hard-won practicalities.** Freight is charged on **volumetric weight** (167 kg per m³ air), so **crate dimensions, not bike weight, set the price** — removing the screen and sometimes the front wheel measurably cuts cost. Crating timber must be **ISPM15-treated** (the international phytosanitary standard for wood packaging) or you risk confiscation. And sea freight "requires your physical presence for customs clearance in most countries," with delays in week-long units, so destination storage fees can eat the savings — air freight is preferred for bridge legs precisely because it is predictable.

**One structural warning:** **Malaysia requires a carnet to import a motorcycle by sea** even though it does not require one to enter by land. If you enter Malaysia overland without getting the carnet stamped and then try to ship out, you have a problem. **Get it stamped in on entry if there is any chance you will ship out.**

### The Port Klang ↔ Tanjung Balai route, and why it matters more than it looks

Getting from Indonesia to Malaysia is harder than it should be. **The Dumai–Melaka ferry is passenger-only and will not carry motorbikes**, confirmed by a rider's failed 2018 attempt; the Belawan–Penang ro-ro is long gone. Exactly two options work:

**Option 1 — Port Klang ⇄ Tanjung Balai (Asahan), Sumatra, by cargo boat.** Agents Azwan (+60 13303 1591, Malaysia) and Tony (+62 823 6008 5666, Indonesia). About **RM 1,850 / IDR 5.5m / USD 350** all-in including taxes and clearance. The carnet is checked and stamped on the Indonesian side; hand it to the agent in advance. **The rider cannot travel with the bike** — you take the fast ferry or fly Medan–KL; the bike leaves in the evening and arrives cleared about when you land. *(MEDIUM-HIGH, reports from November 2022 onward)*

**Note the significance for your central question:** this route structurally separates rider from bike, and customs on both sides handle it as routine. That is direct evidence that a carnet bike moving without its rider is normal in this region.

**Option 2 — the Borneo land border** at Aruk ("the easiest of the two") or Entikong/Tebedu. Carnet stamped out of Indonesia and into Malaysia, insurance bought, then the ICP — **but there may be no JPJ office at the remote border, so you may have to get the ICP in Kuching.** Requires reaching Kalimantan first. *(MEDIUM-HIGH)*

## Part 6 — What I would actually do

Taking the research as a whole, here is the shape of a trip that works with your constraint rather than against it.

**Design principle: never fly home from inside a short clock.** Every flight out should happen in one of four situations — inside a permit with months of margin, immediately after riding across a border into a fresh jurisdiction, from a country with a capped or non-escalating penalty (Georgia, Thailand), or under a formal clock-stopping mechanism (Greek immobilisation, Turkish customs surrender, a bonded warehouse).

**Before you leave Australia**, in rough order of importance:

1. **Ring the AAA** (+61 2 6247 7311). Get the current fee schedule, the actual security terms for India and Pakistan, whether they courier replacement carnets abroad, and the extension procedure from overseas. Buy the **larger page-count booklet.**
2. **Get a written ABF or ATO position on GST on the returning bike.** This is a AUD 1,500–2,000 question and Schedule 4 item 17's absence from the ATO's non-taxable list is unresolved.
3. **Get Service NSW to confirm in writing** that an overseas government-authorised inspection (PUSPAKOM in Malaysia, Thai DLT, Georgian technical inspection) satisfies the overseas renewal pathway. **If NSW rego lapses, your EU temporary admission relief fails simultaneously in every European country.** This is the biggest single structural risk to the whole plan and it sits entirely in Australia.
4. **Write to Bea Cukai** naming Mota'ain and your carnet, and secure written confirmation that you will be cleared under PMK 228/2014, not the PPLB regime. Hold a Plan B of shipping Darwin→Surabaya.
5. **Ask AAM Malaysia** whether JPJ will issue a fresh ICP to a bike that has sat in a Malaysian workshop without re-entering the country.
6. **Put the EU six-months question in writing to German or Dutch customs**: does exiting the EU customs territory reset Article 217's six months, or is it six-in-twelve? This decides whether Balkan-hopping works.
7. **Read the Caravanistan forum threads yourself** — long-term storage in the Eurasian Customs Union, parking a vehicle in Iran on a carnet, the Caspian ferry page. Nobody could open them for you and they go straight to your question.
8. **Assemble the evidence pack** for the eventual return: export declaration, pre-departure photographs from every angle, odometer, registration papers.

**On the route itself**, the parking plan that falls out of the research:

- **Short gaps (2–8 weeks) in Asia: Chiang Mai.** Bike Tour Asia at 1,500 THB/month, seven kilometres from the airport, or HiVolt at 850. Extend the customs TIP while you are in the country. Worst case is a 10,000 THB fine — but mind the red-flag warning and do not make a habit of exiting with the bike inside on an expired permit.
- **Longer gaps in Asia: ride to Malaysia first.** Better clock, real freight infrastructure, government-authorised inspection regime for your NSW rego, and Dietrich Logistics combining storage with the ability to ship if plans change. **Within Malaysia, base in Penang rather than Kuala Lumpur**: Georgetown is 150 km from the Thai border, which turns the ride-it-out clock reset into a morning instead of a two-day round trip. Accept that flying home means connecting through KL or Singapore, take the Penang storage market as thinner and unevidenced compared with Petaling Jaya, and settle the ICP question with JPJ in writing first, because since March 2026 the stated penalty for a foreign vehicle without a valid ICP more than two kilometres from the border is **seizure**.
- **Anything in Central Asia: Osh, and keep it under six months.** Silkway Wheels or AlaaToo Moto. The May 2025 Kyrgyz rule means a stay approaching six months requires the bike to physically leave for 30 days, which you cannot do from Sydney. Note Osh's thin flight connections.
- **Everything west of the Caspian: Georgia, and stop worrying.** Ninety days, a 1,000 GEL total cap, no procedure, a year of visa-free stay for you, StoreSafe in Tbilisi, and Poti and Batumi for onward freight. This is where a multi-season trip should be based.
- **A genuinely long stop (6–24 months) in Europe: Greece, via myAADE immobilisation.** The only purpose-built door in the EU. Sort out a Greek tax number in advance and resolve the third-party guarantee question.
- **Turkey: only with the Ek-7 filed in person, only for short absences, and never let the overstay reach three months.** Clear your HGS balance before the bike leaves.
- **Never: China, Turkmenistan, Tajikistan, Vietnam, Iran, Cambodia, Kosovo, Montenegro.**

**And the honest strategic point.** Your trip design — ride a leg, park it, fly home, come back — is fundamentally incompatible with the two most expensive segments on the map. China cannot be paused at any price. Iran cannot be entered. Between them they sit across the middle of the route. That makes the **Chennai–Bangkok sea freight at USD 2,330** and the **Tbilisi–Baku truck workaround** not fallbacks but plausibly the primary plan: cheaper than a solo China permit, infinitely more flexible, and they deliver you to the two best parking countries on earth years earlier than the overland line would.

## Part 7 — What I do not know

I would rather hand you a short list of honest holes than a long list of confident guesses. In rough order of how much they matter:

1. **Does the EU's six months reset when you exit the customs territory, or is it six-in-twelve?** No official source states it either way. Decides whether Balkan-hopping works.
2. **Can a temporary import permit be extended by an agent or a power of attorney while the owner is abroad — anywhere?** No source answered this in any country. The EAEU's "actual possession and use of the customs declarant" language cuts against it. This single unknown constrains every long absence. **Partly opened since: Nepal's TIV system, launched May 2026, appears to allow an online extension, described as available "from anywhere within Nepal." Whether it can be filed from outside Nepal is now the single highest-value question on this list, because it is the only online renewal path found on the entire route.**
3. **India: is the passport endorsed, and can you leave the bike at all?** One specialist secondary source says no; one fifteen-year-old firsthand account says yes. No 2020s evidence either way. Ask Seco Shipping and FIAA in writing.
4. **Does Turkey's customs surrender actually stop the clock, and is it three months or six?** Turkish and English official sources disagree, and the whole viability of Turkey for you turns on it.
5. **Will JPJ issue a fresh ICP to a bike parked in Malaysia that never re-entered?** **Substantially answered, and downgraded from unknown to thinly evidenced:** a rider who stored a bike six months in Kuala Lumpur obtained ICP and insurance inland through AAM at Shah Alam in April 2019, then shipped out. One case, predating the March 2026 tightening, so confirm it still holds. The live sub-questions are now: does the ICP period follow the insurance period; does "180 days in one year" mean a calendar year or a rolling twelve months; does stored, non-circulating time count against the ceiling; and can AAM Penang do inland issuance, or only Shah Alam? All four in one email, and note that since **10 March 2026** the stated penalty for a foreign vehicle without a valid ICP more than two kilometres from the border road is **seizure**.
6. **Does a carnet override Nepal's brand-new 30-day TIV cap?** TIV launched in the first week of May 2026 and nobody has published an experience under it. Determines whether Nepal is a 30-day dash or a six-month base. **Narrowed: Nepal's published customs position still states six months in twelve with a carnet against 30 days without, and TIV's own coverage notes name third-country vehicles explicitly, so the two read as parallel systems rather than one replacing the other. Still nobody has published a third-country carnet cleared through TIV.**
7. **GST on the returning bike.** AUD 1,500–2,000, unresolved.
8. **Will Service NSW accept a foreign government-authorised inspection?** The rule is clear and official; the practice has never been demonstrated by any overlander I could find.
9. **Current AAA fees and the actual India/Pakistan security terms.** The only figures found are from a 2018 form.
10. **Cost of every bonded-warehouse and clock-stopping option** — Turkish customs custody, EU customs warehousing, UK bonded storage, Greek immobilisation fees, StoreSafe Tbilisi. **Not one provider anywhere publishes a rate for a motorcycle.**
11. **Whether Iran will be enterable at all**, and whether the 250cc motorcycle rule is live.
12. **Armenia's temporary import duration.** No source gave a number.
13. **Kazakhstan's temporary import duration.** Same.
14. **How a Kazakh/EAEU temporary import is discharged on a westbound Caspian sailing.** The exact paperwork event that could leave an open liability.
15. **Whether the Kazakhstan–Russia and Russia–Georgia borders are usable by an Australian in a private vehicle in 2026.**
16. **Whether Australians need a Mongolian visa in 2026**, which may determine the vehicle slip's duration.
17. **Whether continental third-party products (ADAC, Alessie) cover the UK post-Brexit.**
18. **Real cost of a Vietnam Decree 30 escorted entry**, and of Central Asia → Georgia air freight.
19. **Whether the Mae Sot–Myawaddy reopening of 28 May 2026 extends to private vehicles.** Reported only as a cargo trial. Re-check three months before you would need it.
20. **Azerbaijan's status after 1 July 2026.**

**Two structural warnings to carry with you.** First, the favourable evidence in this report skews 2012–2019, and enforcement across the whole route has been tightening since 2020 — Greece immobilising, Kosovo recording, Montenegro registering, Kyrgyzstan capping, Pakistan monitoring. Second, because Myanmar and Wagah are shut, almost nobody is doing what you are about to do, so in several countries you will be the precedent rather than following one. Neither is a reason not to go. Both are reasons to get things in writing, to photograph every counterfoil at every border, and to send a short letter to the customs temporary-imports unit before every flight home.
